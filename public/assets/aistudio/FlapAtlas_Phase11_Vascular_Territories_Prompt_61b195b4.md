## 0. Purpose and the one rule that matters most: NO CLUTTER
Source text: Morris SF, Taylor GI. *Vascular territories*. In: Neligan PC, *Plastic Surgery*, Volume 1 (Principles), Chapter 23 (pp. 321–345). The user's PDF of this chapter is the only new clinical source for this phase. Use it to add the missing vascular-territory knowledge (angiosomes, perforator territories, venosomes, neurovascular relationships, flap-design principles) **on top of the existing 3D model without changing how the model looks when the mode is off**.

Anti-clutter contract (every item is an acceptance test):
1. **One mode at a time.** A single "Vascular Territories" mode switch with exactly one active sub-mode: `Off | Angiosomes | Perforator corridors | Venosomes | Choke & delay`. Entering a mode snapshots the current layer state; leaving it restores that snapshot exactly.
2. **Auto-declutter on entry.** Skin → translucent (carries the territory colours), organs hidden, all procedural perforator markers / flap overlays / dashed design lines hidden except the selected flap, real vessel meshes limited to the *source arteries of the selected territory* (all other arteries/veins dimmed to ~8 % or hidden), muscles and bones ghosted unless a territory is selected.
3. **Colour is never the only code.** 40+ territories cannot be told apart by hue alone: use region-family hues (head/neck, trunk, upper limb, lower limb) + lightness steps + computed boundary outlines + on-demand labels. Respect the existing Okabe-Ito colour-blind toggle.
4. **Labels on demand.** No permanent labels. Show a label only for hovered/tapped territory (max 1) or for the selected flap's territories (max 4), with a leader line and collision avoidance.
5. **Focus mode.** Tapping a territory focuses it: its colour stays, all others desaturate to neutral grey with only a thin outline. A "Show neighbours" chip adds its adjacent territories at 35 % strength.
6. **Phone-first UI.** The user works on a phone. Controls live in one collapsible bottom sheet (mode chips, opacity slider, depth slider, legend) — no extra side panels. Touch: tap = identify, long-press = pin label. Keep ≥ 44 px targets.
7. **Original artwork only.** Do NOT embed, trace, or reproduce any figure from the book (Manchot, Salmon, Taylor colour maps, radiographs, perforator maps). All overlays are computed/drawn by the app. Text from the chapter is paraphrased briefly with a citation (chapter, page, figure/table).
8. **Honest tagging.** Every territory, corridor or marker that is not derived from a real BP3D mesh carries the existing tag `"Schematic — not from source model"`; computed regions additionally carry `"Computed approximation — individual anatomy varies; confirm with Doppler / CTA"`.

Definition (p. 321): an angiosome is a composite block of tissue (skin, fascia, muscle, bone, nerve) supplied by one source artery and its accompanying vein(s); adjacent angiosomes are linked by reduced-caliber **choke vessels** or by **true anastomoses** (no reduction in caliber). The body is a 3-D jigsaw; the text states the territories were later subdivided (61 territories, p. 329; perforator atlas, Fig 23.51) and that the head and neck, first mapped as eight, comprise 13 (p. 329).
Each entry gets, from `angiosome_bp3d_map.json` (Appendix A, verified against the BP3D 4.0 parts list): `bp3d_coverage` (`full | partial | none`), the exact BP3D artery names with **left/right/midline FJ ids**, and for `partial`/`none` a plain-language `schematic_hint`. Resolve FJ ids through `extras.fj` of the loaded nodes; if an id is missing in the loaded files, downgrade that seed to schematic and list it in the QA report. Facts about coverage (verified): 24 full, 5 partial, 12 none. Absent from BP3D: facial, internal maxillary, superficial temporal, occipital, posterior auricular, superior thyroid, superior/inferior gluteal, profunda femoris, adductor (profunda), sural, peroneal (fibular), deep circumflex iliac, internal pudendal, and the posterior interosseous artery itself. BP3D has a single `femoral artery` (not split into common/superficial) — territories 31 and 32 share that mesh; show them as one "femoral (31+32)" region unless the user splits them by calibration.

### 2.3 `muscleTerritories.ts` — muscle angiosome counts and neurovascular types
**Leg (Table 23.2, p. 332.e5):** one territory — tibialis anterior, extensor digitorum longus, extensor hallucis longus, peroneus tertius; two — peroneus longus, peroneus brevis, flexor digitorum longus, flexor hallucis longus; three — gastrocnemius, soleus, popliteus, tibialis posterior. Leg angiosomes: anterior tibial, posterior tibial, peroneal, sural, popliteal (Figs 23.30–23.33). Anterior compartment is supplied exclusively by the anterior tibial artery (vulnerable to ischaemia; only connections to the posterior tibial territory are via periosteum and the cutaneous network). Gastrocnemius is supplied by the medial and lateral sural arteries (one per head, little overlap); soleus by many short branches of posterior tibial, popliteal and peroneal vessels with free intramuscular anastomoses. Junctions between angiosomes lie mainly *within* tissues, especially muscles, rather than between them.
**Forearm (Figs 23.26–23.29, p. 332.e1–e3):** five angiosomes — brachial, radial, ulnar, anterior interosseous, posterior interosseous (plus recurrent arteries: radial recurrent, ulnar recurrent, interosseous recurrent, and ulnar collateral / profunda brachii proximally). The only muscles lying solely within the radial angiosome are brachioradialis, ECRL and ECRB (via the radial recurrent artery, which anastomoses with the profunda brachii). Flexor digitorum profundus and flexor carpi ulnaris are supplied only by the ulnar artery or recurrent interosseous branches; flexor digitorum superficialis is better protected (extra radial contribution). Cutaneous perforators emerge in rows along the radial and ulnar arteries; proximal forearm perforators are mostly musculocutaneous.
**Table 23.1 (p. 329) neurovascular classification** → show as a badge `Type I–IV` on the muscle detail card (only for muscles that exist as BP3D meshes; for absent muscles show the badge on the schematic card): Type I (single unbranched motor nerve) — latissimus dorsi, extensor indicis, extensor pollicis longus, abductor pollicis longus, palmaris longus, teres minor, extensor hallucis longus, plantaris, popliteus. Type II (single branched nerve entering the muscle — the nerve divides before entering) — deltoid, gluteus maximus, trapezius, vastus lateralis, serratus anterior, flexor carpi ulnaris, biceps brachii, brachialis, flexor pollicis longus, flexor hallucis longus, pectineus, adductor longus, adductor brevis. Type III (multiple branches from the same nerve trunk) — gastrocnemius, sartorius, tibialis anterior, flexor digitorum superficialis, subscapularis, teres major, triceps, extensor carpi ulnaris, extensor digitorum longus, gluteus medius, gluteus minimus, vastus medialis, vastus intermedius, peroneus longus, soleus, tibialis posterior. Type IV (multiple branches from different nerve trunks) — rectus abdominis, levator scapulae, internal oblique, digastric, erector spinae. Clinical meaning (p. 328): in type I the nerve divides only after entering the muscle and several vascular pedicles supply a continuous network, so a vascularised segment can be removed with its nerve while viable muscle stays in situ; types III and IV can be subdivided into separate neurovascular functional units (e.g. one head of gastrocnemius); serratus anterior, latissimus dorsi, gracilis and rectus femoris are often used this way, taking a portion of the muscle with its motor nerve and blood supply; several type IV muscles are formed by fusion of adjacent somites (rectus abdominis, internal oblique); each motor nerve is accompanied by a vascular pedicle but not the reverse, and the nerve usually travels with the dominant pedicle (exception: sternocleidomastoid).
**Venous types of muscle (p. 326, Fig 23.14):** Type I — one venous territory draining one direction; Type II — two territories draining in opposite directions, linked by oscillating avalvular veins; Type III — three or more territories (e.g. supraspinatus type I, gracilis type II, sartorius type III as shown in the figure).

### 2.4 `perforatorFacts.ts` (all with page refs)
- ≈ 400 cutaneous perforators ≥ 0.5 mm per body (p. 323–324, 341); ≈ 40 % direct / 60 % indirect (p. 324); ≈ 60 % musculocutaneous / 40 % septocutaneous (p. 341). Direct = pierce deep fascia between deep tissues (axial and septocutaneous); indirect = terminal muscular branches that penetrate muscle (musculocutaneous, e.g. DIEA perforators). Direct cutaneous vessels arise (1) from a source artery just beneath the deep fascia (e.g. superficial inferior epigastric), (2) as a direct continuation of the source artery (cutaneous branches of the external carotid), (3) from a deeply situated source artery or its muscle branch and follow intermuscular septa (septocutaneous branches of the lateral circumflex femoral artery).
- Size/density: large, long, few in head, neck, torso, arm, thigh; smaller, shorter, more numerous in forearm, leg, hands, feet; highest density of small perforators in palms and soles (fixed skin).
- Where they emerge: fixed skin sites — perimeter of muscles / intermuscular septa, flexor surfaces of joints, near dorsal and ventral midlines, base of skull, orbital margins, nostrils, parotid, facial creases, borders of trapezius/SCM, hyoid, clavicle/sternum, subcutaneous border of tibia. "Arteries radiate from fixed to mobile areas; veins converge from mobile to fixed" (Box 23.1, p. 334).
- Leg perforator lines (p. 332.e4): anterior — between tibialis anterior and extensor digitorum longus (anterior tibial), between flexor digitorum longus and soleus (posterior tibial); lateral — peroneal septocutaneous, distal ones easiest to dissect; proximal peroneal perforators often have long intramuscular courses.
- **Fig 23.51 abbreviation list (p. 343) — the only authorised perforator-territory abbreviations** (use them as `perforasomeCode`): AIA anterior interosseous a.; ATA anterior tibial a.; BA brachial a.; CSA circumflex scapular a.; DCA dorsal carpal arch; DCIA deep circumflex iliac a.; DGA descending genicular a.; DIEA deep inferior epigastric a.; DPA dorsalis pedis a.; DPAA deep palmar arch; DPIA dorsal branch of posterior intercostal a.; EPA external pudendal a.; FA facial a.; IGA inferior gluteal a.; IOA infraorbital a.; IPA internal pudendal a.; ITA internal thoracic (mammary) a.; IUCA inferior ulnar collateral a.; LA lumbar aa.; LCA lateral calcaneal a.; LCFA lateral circumflex femoral a.; LIGA lateral inferior genicular a.; LPA lateral plantar a.; LPIA lateral branches of posterior intercostal a.; LSA lateral sural a.; LSGA lateral superior genicular a.; LTA lateral thoracic (mammary) a.; MA mental a.; MCA medial calcaneal a.; MCFA medial circumflex femoral a.; MIGA medial inferior genicular a.; MSA medial sural a.; MSGA medial superior genicular a.; OCA occipital a.; OPA ophthalmic a.; PA popliteal a.; PAURA posterior auricular a.; PBA profunda brachii a.; PCHA posterior circumflex humeral a.; PFA profunda femoris a.; PIA posterior interosseous a.; PNA peroneal a.; PRCA posterior radial collateral a.; PTA posterior tibial a.; RA radial a.; RRA radial recurrent a.; SCIA superficial circumflex iliac a.; SEA superior epigastric a.; SGA superior gluteal a.; SIEA superficial inferior epigastric a.; SMA submental a.; SPA superficial palmar arch; STA superficial temporal a.; STHA superior thyroid a.; SUCA superior ulnar collateral a.; TAA thoracoacromial a. Labels that appear in the figure but are not defined in its caption must NOT be used as codes.
- **Perforator flap nomenclature (Fig 23.52, p. 343; p. 342):** `<source artery>` + `AP` (artery perforator) + `-<muscle initials>` for the muscle the perforator passes through; suffix `-s` = septocutaneous. Examples in the text: DIEAP-ra (rectus abdominis), LCFAP-vl (ALT, vastus lateralis), SMAP (submental), PIOAP-s, TAP-ld (thoracodorsal, latissimus dorsi), SGAP-gm, IGAP-gm.

### 2.5 `designPrinciples.ts` — flap-design concepts (cards; each paraphrased, one sentence + page ref)
Box 23.1 (p. 334): vessels follow the connective-tissue framework; arteries radiate from fixed to mobile areas and veins converge from mobile to fixed; vessels "hitchhike" with nerves; vessel size and orientation are products of tissue growth and differentiation (Fig 23.39); vessels interconnect as a continuous 3-D network of arcades (Roman-aqueduct tiers, p. 321, 336); vessels obey the **law of equilibrium** (inverse size of adjacent territories, e.g. superficial inferior epigastric vs deep inferior epigastric; a large SIEV implies the lower part of a DIEAP flap may depend on superficial venous drainage, p. 337); vessels have a relatively constant destination but variable origin (SIEA/SCIA may arise from the common femoral artery or a common trunk); venous networks consist of linked valvular and avalvular channels; muscles are the prime movers of venous return.
Flap survival and design (pp. 331–332, 339–344): each angiosome is the safe boundary in each tissue layer; the junction between adjacent angiosomes usually lies *within* muscle, giving a bypass shunt; most muscles span ≥ 2 angiosomes so skin of the adjacent territory can be captured through the muscle; a flap can reliably capture the adjacent cutaneous territory in any direction; in ≈ 80 % of animal experiments the limit of survival was the junction of the second and third territory; a third territory can potentially be captured after a delay. Survival length depends on (1) caliber and length of the dominant vessels, (2) caliber and span of the adjacent captured arteries/veins, (3) caliber and length of the connecting choke vessels, (4) favourable vs unfavourable venous return, plus flap volume. Large widely-spaced perforators (loose skin of torso, scalp) → large territories → long flaps; small close perforators (sole) → short viable length.
Fasciocutaneous / musculocutaneous (pp. 340–341): include deep fascia where skin is relatively fixed to fascia (scalp, extremities); unnecessary where skin is mobile (iliac fossa, breast); skin firmly bound to muscle (gluteus maximus, latissimus dorsi) is reliably supplied through the muscle, whereas over muscle that glides beneath fascia (gracilis) the cutaneous supply is tenuous; "septocutaneous" can be a surgical rather than anatomical term (radial/ulnar flaps dissected in loose areolar envelope); in the lateral upper calf the course of peroneal perforators is variable. Distally based flaps: perforators radiate in stellate patterns, veins converge on the pedicle, so a distally based flap contains arteries and valved veins directed back to that perforator; large longitudinal subcutaneous veins of the limbs drain proximally based flaps favourably (valves oriented that way) whereas in the lower abdomen superficial inferior epigastric veins drain distally toward the groin (unfavourable for proximally based abdominal flaps); scalp veins are mostly valveless. For musculocutaneous flaps the venous pathway depends on muscle type: type I drains favourably regardless of skin-paddle position; over the distal territory of type II/III muscles the valves oppose the draining pedicle (problem highlighted in the pedicled TRAM, p. 341).
**Propeller flap (Fig 23.50, p. 340):** a local island perforator flap likened to propeller blades, designed on the largest adjacent uninjured perforator to the wound, with `a = b + c + 1 cm` as printed in the figure legend; the portion b closes the donor site and a covers the wound; release the deep fascia around the perforator to avoid kinking. (Show as a read-only rule card; do not turn it into an interactive calculator that outputs clinical numbers.)
**Composite flaps (p. 344):** angiosome tissues interconnect between layers — the direct cutaneous perforators of the SCIA interconnect with indirect perforators of the DCIA; a DCIA osteocutaneous flap perfuses the SCIA skin territory, and the reverse perfuses the anterior iliac crest segment and attached muscles.
**Delay (pp. 342–344, Figs 23.53–23.56):** options — partial incision around the flap margin, ligation of non-pedicle vessels, partial/complete elevation, possibly in stages. Physiology as printed: immediate vasoconstriction, gradual dilation over the first 48 h, dramatic dilation 48–72 h, plateau by ~7 days; the enlargement is permanent and irreversible; necrosis usually occurs at the next choke anastomosis or the one beyond.
**Neurovascular relationships (pp. 326–328, 335; Figs 23.15–23.16, 23.46):** each cutaneous nerve is accompanied by an artery but not always the same way — five patterns (A) a long artery joined to its neighbour by a true anastomosis courses with the nerve; (B) a chain-linked system of arteries hitchhikes with the nerve; (C) nerve and artery pierce the deep fascia together; (D) the nerve first runs parallel to an artery, then approaches the neighbouring artery and descends along its branches; (E) the nerve crosses the primary and secondary arcades of the artery before running parallel to the vascular network. Superficial veins follow cutaneous nerves (horizontal subdermal network), deep veins follow arteries. Examples named in the text: lateral intercostal neurovascular perforators on the torso; saphenous system of the lower limb; lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh and the superficial circumflex iliac artery; the long and short saphenous flaps (Pontén) and the sural/saphenous neurovascular flaps. Upper-limb cutaneous nerves labelled in Fig 23.46: axillary, lower lateral brachial, supraclavicular, intercostobrachial, posterior antebrachial, medial antebrachial, medial brachial, lateral antebrachial, dorsal branch of ulnar, superficial radial, median, ulnar. BP3D has almost no peripheral nerves, so these are **educational pattern cards only** — draw no fake nerve geometry on the body.

## 3. 11b — Territory field engine (the technical core)
Angiosomes are 3-D blocks (skin → fascia → muscle → bone), so compute **one 3-D territory field** and let every tissue layer sample it; do not paint separate 2-D maps.
1. **Seeds.** For each angiosome and side: (a) *mesh seeds* — sample the real BP3D artery meshes listed in Appendix A (vertices subsampled to ≈ 5 mm spacing, using the loaded geometry in shared BP3D coordinates); (b) *schematic seeds* for `partial`/`none` entries — build short polyline/capsule seeds from the `schematic_hint` using calibrated anchors (ASIS, PSIS, greater trochanter, acromion, inferior scapular angle, mastoid, umbilicus, fibular head, lateral malleolus, patella, etc.) plus extra landmarks auto-proposed from the BP3D skeleton/skin when needed (e.g. ischial tuberosity, medial malleolus, olecranon, medial canthus, mental protuberance, vertex). Mirror left↔right across the calibrated midline. Seeds from schematic hints are drawn with hatched fill and the schematic tag.
2. **Field.** Bake a `Data3DTexture` voxel grid (target ≈ 8 mm voxels over the body bounding box; RGBA8: primary territory id, secondary id, boundary-distance, tissue-class flag) in a Web Worker. Rule: each voxel inside the body envelope (skin mesh interior) is assigned to the seed with the smallest *weighted* distance `d(v, seed)/w_territory` (a weighted Voronoi, the "law of equilibrium" analogue: bigger weight = bigger territory). The weights are **visual tuning constants** stored in `visualTuning` (default 1.0), clearly labelled "not clinical measurements", adjustable only in Editor mode. Add anatomical barriers by region masks: head/neck/trunk/limb families so a limb territory never leaks across the trunk and a left territory never leaks across the midline except for the midline-crossing structures named in §2.2 (scalp, lips, forehead). Territories 11–13 of §2.2 and other deep-only entries are assigned only to non-skin voxels.
3. **Boundary-distance channel** gives soft/dotted "choke zones" (Choke & delay mode) and outlines.
4. **Caching.** Compute once per model version; cache in IndexedDB as `territoryField-bp3d-4.0-v1` with progress UI ("Building vascular territory field…") and a graceful fallback (per-vertex colours on a 2×-subdivided copy of the skin) when `Data3DTexture`/WebGL2 is unavailable. Never block first paint; the mode becomes available when the field is ready. Cap memory; dispose worker/buffers.
5. **Sampling.** A small ShaderMaterial patch (`onBeforeCompile`) on skin, muscle and bone materials reads the field with the fragment's world position, so skin, muscle and bone are coloured consistently and the junctional zone appears *inside* muscles, as the chapter describes. Use a separate render pass/layer for outlines; keep draw calls unchanged.
6. **Calibration report (Editor mode).** For the muscles in §2.3 (leg Table 23.2, head-neck Table 23.3) count the distinct territories their voxels intersect and compare with the book's count; list mismatches as "territory calibration warnings" — do NOT auto-fix and do NOT hide them.
7. Honest limits to show in the About card: BodyParts3D is one adult male reference body (99 % polygon-reduced); territories are an approximation computed from real source-artery geometry where available and from anatomical hints elsewhere; they are not patient-specific.

## 6. 11e — Perforator corridors & planner
- **Corridors, not fake points.** The chapter publishes a perforator map as a figure only (no coordinates), so do not place hundreds of invented dots. Instead draw, per selected source artery that has a real mesh, a thin dotted *emergence corridor* on the skin: the projection of the artery's centreline onto the nearest skin along the surface normal, labelled `"Expected perforator zone — schematic; individual perforators vary"`. Apply the printed rules for emphasis (larger/fewer proximally in torso/thigh/arm; smaller/more numerous in forearm/leg/hand/foot; rows along radial/ulnar arteries; anterior and lateral leg septal lines). Instanced meshes only; cluster/hide at far zoom.
- **Perforasome concept (Fig 23.25, p. 333):** for the *selected flap's own perforators* (existing flap data) draw each perforator's soft territory glow — size is a decorative constant tagged `"Schematic extent — not a measurement"`, and the glows tile like a jigsaw around the pedicle's perforators. The app adds no calculation, no recommendation.

## 7. 11f — Venosomes, choke vessels, delay
- **Venosomes:** the chapter states the venous territories parallel the arterial ones (Fig 23.24 uses the same numbering) but with differences.
